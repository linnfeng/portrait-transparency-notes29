# Clearer Choices for AI Portraits

We ask makers of AI portrait tools to make consent, disclosure and pricing information easy to find at the point where someone creates and shares an image. A playful portrait can be a thoughtful gift or an illustration for a creative project. It can also be misunderstood when a viewer assumes that a picture is a photograph, that its subject approved a public use, or that a free preview includes every possible export. These are different questions, and the interface should help people distinguish them before an image is distributed.

This is a request for clearer product communication and responsible review, not a report of personal injury, a claim that every provider behaves in the same way, or a demand that creative tools be removed. I am associated with the Caricature Maker project, and AI assistance helped draft this petition. The examples are hypothetical. I do not claim to speak for an independent research group, a regulator, or all artists and subjects.

The decision makers we are addressing are designers and operators of browser-based portrait generators. They can improve the information surrounding a creative workflow even when the technology itself continues to change. The request is practical: show concise guidance before upload, explain relevant sharing and cost choices before a user commits, and make it easy to find fuller terms without burying the essential distinction in a long document.

A reference image is not just another design asset. It may show a person who has agreed to a private experiment but not to a public profile, an advertisement, or an image shared with a large audience. A tool cannot know the complete relationship between the uploader and the person depicted. It can, however, remind the uploader to obtain appropriate permission and to avoid providing unnecessary personal information. That reminder is more useful when it appears before a photo is submitted rather than only after an output is produced.

We are not asking an interface to promise that every user has obtained permission. A checkbox cannot prove a person's agreement, and a provider should not suggest otherwise. The goal is to present a clear prompt that helps the uploader pause and consider the planned use. The prompt should distinguish permission to use a source photo from permission to publish a resulting illustration. If the use later changes, the people involved may need to discuss it again.

A second request concerns the status of the generated image. A stylized portrait is an interpretation, not a verified record of what a person looks like or what they said. A small label, an optional caption, or an export note can help communicate that an image was generated or substantially altered when that context matters. Such a note should be understandable to an ordinary reader and should not falsely imply that an image has received a formal authenticity certification.

Consider a hypothetical birthday card. A friend might agree to a cartoon picture for a private message, while a later public post may reach people who were never part of the original conversation. The image itself may be harmless, but an accompanying caption could imply a real endorsement or an event that did not occur. The sender should review the image and the surrounding words together. A product can support this by making it easy to understand which output will be shared and by reminding the user that a caption can change the meaning of a picture.

We also ask for clear choices about the source material. A user should be able to understand whether a photo is uploaded, what options exist for deleting or managing it, and where to find the current privacy policy. Providers may have different architectures and retention practices, so a single universal retention number would be inappropriate without evidence. We ask each provider to describe its own actual practice accurately, including meaningful limitations, rather than relying on vague assurances that a file is automatically private.

Cost information deserves the same clarity. Some tools offer a limited free generation, some use credits, and others require payment for particular sizes or exports. Users should be able to identify the relevant limit before spending a credit or encountering a paywall. The distinction between a free trial and an unlimited free service should be visible. Any description of rights to use or redistribute an output should likewise reflect the current terms, not a generalized promise that every image can be used for any purpose.

For transparency, the project with which I am associated is [https://caricature-maker.online/](https://caricature-maker.online/) . Its site describes one free 1K generation, with paid options for additional use. This mention identifies my connection; it is not independent endorsement or evidence that the requested practices have already been implemented. Features, prices, rights and privacy information can change, and anyone considering a tool should consult its current terms. We are not asking readers to buy a product or to accept an unsupported claim about its performance.

Clarity should extend to the draft itself. An image generator can make unexpected changes to glasses, hair, clothing, background objects, or lettering. People reviewing a result benefit from seeing the image at the actual size and crop intended for its destination. A small icon and a printed poster expose different problems. Providers could give users a straightforward way to compare a draft with the intended context and explain that a visual match is not proof of consent or identity.

The resulting information should be accessible and proportionate. Essential guidance should use ordinary language, be readable on a small screen, and avoid forcing people to navigate through unrelated marketing. A short explanation can link to fuller documentation for anyone who needs details. Such an approach does not replace broader accessibility testing, legal advice, or a conversation with the person depicted. It simply makes important choices easier to notice at the moment they are relevant.

There is room for diverse creative expression. We are not asking providers to ban caricature, humor, or fictional characters. An effective caricature can be affectionate, critical, or imaginative depending on its context. The request is for honest boundaries: show users where permission and disclosure matter, make cost and output rights understandable, and avoid representing a generated picture as proof of a person's endorsement or conduct.

A provider responding to this petition could begin with a public checklist describing its own implementation. For example, it could identify where consent guidance appears, where users can find the current data handling information, and how the interface distinguishes a free allowance from a paid option. If an item is not yet available, the provider could say so rather than implying completion. A dated explanation gives users a way to understand the state of a product without turning an aspirational policy into a claim of present compliance.

We invite other people to consider the request on its merits, including artists, people depicted in portraits, accessibility practitioners, and product teams. Their experiences and priorities may differ. A careful discussion should make space for corrections and should avoid claiming consensus merely because an idea sounds reasonable. The objective is not to substitute a generic checklist for an individual person's preferences, but to make the first questions easier to ask.

Please support clearer, timely explanations of consent, AI-assisted imagery, source handling, and costs in portrait-generation workflows. Ask providers to describe what their own tools actually do, update that information when practices change, and give users a meaningful chance to review the intended use before sharing. A creative image can remain playful while the decisions around it are transparent and respectful.
